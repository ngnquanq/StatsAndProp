# Paper Summary: An Analysis of Scam Baiting Calls: Identifying and Extracting Scam Stages and Scripts

**Citation:** Ian D. Wood, Michal Kepkowski, Leron Zinatullin, Travis Darnley, and Mohamed Ali Kaafar, arXiv:2307.01965, 2023; inspected PDF also labels the work as NDSS Symposium 2024.  
**Source:** `/home/nhatquang/Desktop/HCMUS Course/PhD_Applications/Macquarie_Kaafar/kaafar-papers/pdfs/2307.01965-scam-baiting-calls.pdf`  
**One-line takeaway:** The paper shows that scam-baiting call transcripts can be used to semi-automatically recover scam types, scripted stages, social-engineering tactics, and live-call state predictions from phone-scam conversations.

## 1. What Problem Is This Paper Solving?

Phone scams remain hard to defend against because attackers exploit both telephony weaknesses and human susceptibility to persuasive social engineering. Caller ID spoofing, VoIP, cross-jurisdictional operation, and limited global adoption of authentication measures make enforcement and network-level prevention incomplete. Real victim calls are also difficult to study because victims rarely record calls and may hesitate to share sensitive or embarrassing details.

The paper asks how researchers can study scam-call scripts and manipulation strategies at scale when genuine victim data is scarce. The authors use YouTube scam-baiting recordings as a proxy source. These are not genuine victim interactions, but the scammer side is usually authentic until the scammer realizes the call is bait. The aim is to infer the scam type, identify script stages, and understand how scammers move victims toward financial exploitation.

## 2. Main Contributions

- Constructs a dataset of 341 scam-baiting call transcripts, totaling about 90 hours of scam conversations, manually cleaned and annotated by scam type.
- Proposes a semi-automated pipeline combining scam-type annotation, contextualized topic modeling, text-based emotion recognition, and Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) over topic sequences to infer scam-script stages.
- Shows that scam type can be predicted early from scammer utterances: the reported F1 performance is about 85-95% with one utterance and 92-98% with five or six utterances.
- Evaluates live-call scam-stage prediction using RoBERTa-based classifiers trained to predict HMM-inferred stages.
- Maps inferred stages and topics to social-engineering principles such as authority, commitment, reciprocity, liking/familiarity, and distraction.

## 3. Method Explained

**Intuition:** Scam calls often follow scripts: establish a role, explain a problem or opportunity, instruct the victim, then request money, gift cards, banking details, or remote access. If scripts are consistent, scammer utterances should reveal recurring topics and state transitions. The paper models each call as a sequence and infers hidden scam stages from observable language.

**Technical core:**

- **Data sourcing:** The authors searched YouTube for scam-baiting videos and selected recordings with long, coherent scammer conversations, filtering out entertainment-heavy, offensive, or disconnected material.
- **Transcription and cleaning:** Audio was transcribed with Rev.ai. The authors manually assigned utterances to Scammer or Victim and removed non-call material and post-reveal content.
- **Scam-type annotation:** Two authors developed and verified the scam categories. The main categories used for later analysis were social security number, refund, support, and reward scams. Marginal categories such as family member, tax, and charity had too few transcripts for deeper modeling.
- **Topic modeling:** The authors trained a contextualized topic model with 50 topics on scammer utterances. They report high topic diversity and reasonable coherence, then manually labeled topics using top words, highest-scoring utterances, and weighted utterance samples.
- **Emotion extraction:** They applied a RoBERTa-based emotion model trained on public emotion datasets. The model estimates Ekman's six emotions plus neutral from text only.
- **Script extraction:** For each scam type, they converted each utterance to its top topic and trained HMMs to infer hidden stages, then manually interpreted states using topic emissions and representative utterances.
- **Prediction tasks:** They trained RoBERTa-based text classifiers for scam-type prediction and scam-stage prediction in simulated live-call settings where only utterances up to the current point are available.

## 4. Experiments And Results

- **Data:** The final dataset contains 341 transcripts, about 179 total hours of two-party audio and about 90 hours of scammer speech. It includes 30,752 scammer utterances before filtering, with 15,234 final scammer utterances used for analysis. The main categories are social security number, refund, support, and reward/gift-card-like scams; family member, tax, and charity calls are marginal.
- **Topics:** The topic model recovered interpretable themes: legal charges, case IDs, identity-theft stories, cars in Texas with drugs or blood, bank-account checks, gift-card payment, remote-access instructions, website spelling, refund forms, and support-scam computer instructions.
- **HMM stages:** The authors found stage graphs that matched close reading. Refund scams progress through greeting, device check, web-browser instructions, download/install instructions, cancellation/refund options, bank-account steps, chit-chat, and payment instructions. Social security number scams include identity verification, case IDs, legal-charge introduction, detailed criminal scenarios, supervisor redirection, and payment-related states.
- **Manual validation:** Human annotation showed moderate to strong agreement with the HMM states. Cohen's kappa against HMM states was 0.55 strict / 0.66 relaxed for social security number scams, 0.52 / 0.65 for refund, 0.61 / 0.69 for support, and 0.28 / 0.50 for reward.
- **Scam-type prediction:** Binary RoBERTa classifiers for each scam type performed strongly from early utterances. The paper reports 85-95% F1 with one utterance and 92-98% with five or six utterances. Refund and support were harder to distinguish than social security number and reward scams.
- **Scam-stage prediction:** Stage classifiers achieved relaxed accuracies of 0.81 for social security number, 0.83 for refund, 0.70 for support, and 0.91 for reward. Strict accuracies were 0.54, 0.59, 0.47, and 0.50 respectively, with margins over random baselines of roughly 30-53 percentage points depending on scam type and target.
- **Emotion and social engineering:** Scammers were generally less emotional than scam baiters, but emotion varied by stage. The paper highlights anger used to suppress interruption and force compliance, disgust-laden crime-scene descriptions in social security number scams, and familiar or apologetic chit-chat near payment-related stages.

## 5. Limitations And Risks

- Scam-baiting calls are not genuine victim calls. Baiters may exaggerate behavior, derail scripts, prolong conversations, or select calls for entertainment value.
- YouTube videos may be edited, merged, truncated, or include host commentary, so transcript sequence noise can remain after cleaning.
- The sample is biased toward public scam-baiting channels and primarily US-oriented scam types; it is not a comprehensive view of global phone scams.
- Some scam categories have very small sample sizes, especially reward and marginal types, limiting HMM quality and external validity.
- Speech-to-text and diarization errors remain possible, especially when callers talk over each other.
- Emotion recognition is text-only, so it misses prosody, pitch, tone, and other vocal signals that matter in phone persuasion.
- The prediction tasks assume the call is already known to be a scam; the paper does not solve legitimate-call versus scam-call detection.

## 6. Why This Matters For Nguyen Nhat Quang's IAM/Security-ML PhD Direction

This paper is relevant to an IAM/security-ML direction because it treats scam defense as a problem of identity claims, interaction state, and adaptive risk. The scammer impersonates an authority, asks for identity verification, elicits sensitive attributes, and steers the victim through a staged authorization-like workflow: "prove who you are," "confirm the case," "install this tool," "move funds," or "do not tell anyone." That makes the paper useful for human-facing identity abuse, not only technical account compromise.

For security ML, the paper is a good example of combining representation learning with interpretable sequence structure. Topic models provide themes, HMMs provide stage progression, and RoBERTa classifiers provide live-call prediction. This hybrid approach is more useful than a black-box detector alone because it supports analyst understanding, user warnings, education, and possibly conversational anti-scam agents.

It also connects to PhD questions around real-time risk scoring, explainable security interventions, and human-in-the-loop defense: how to detect identity-abuse scripts during live interaction, explain warnings to vulnerable users, and avoid false positives when legitimate service calls share surface features with scams.

## 7. How To Use This Paper

- **Cite it for:** A dataset and methodology for analyzing phone-scam scripts from scam-baiting calls; using topic models plus HMMs to infer scam stages; early scam-type and scam-stage prediction from scammer utterances; evidence that social-engineering tactics appear as structured conversational progressions.
- **Do not cite it for:** Claims about all phone scams, true victim behavior, production-ready scam detection, or audio-prosody-based emotion analysis. The dataset is proxy data from public YouTube scam baiters.
- **Useful framing:** This is a measurement and modeling paper for scam intelligence, not merely a classifier paper. Its strength is the combination of qualitative close reading and automated sequence modeling.
- **Follow-up reading:** Telephony honeypot work, robocall/spam-call detection, scam-signature approaches for phone conversations, vishing social-engineering studies, and real-time warning systems for user-facing security interventions.
