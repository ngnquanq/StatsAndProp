# Paper Summary: A Large-Scale Empirical Analysis of Custom GPTs' Vulnerabilities in the OpenAI Ecosystem

**Citation:** Sunday Oyinlola Ogundoyin, Muhammad Ikram, Hassan Jameel Asghar, Benjamin Zi Hao Zhao, and Dali Kaafar, 2025, arXiv:2505.08148v1.  
**Source:** `/home/nhatquang/Desktop/HCMUS Course/PhD_Applications/Macquarie_Kaafar/kaafar-papers/pdfs/2505.08148-custom-gpt-vulnerabilities.pdf`  
**One-line takeaway:** The paper shows, through large-scale empirical probing, that custom GPTs in the OpenAI ecosystem are widely vulnerable to prompt-based attacks, with risk shaped more by developer configuration and safeguards than by popularity alone.

## 1. What Problem Is This Paper Solving?

The paper studies the security of custom GPTs: GPT-store applications created by third-party developers on top of OpenAI base models with custom instructions, uploaded knowledge, conversation starters, capabilities, and actions. These applications are attractive because they adapt a general model to specialized workflows, but the same customization layer can weaken or bypass built-in safety controls. The authors argue that prior work on custom GPT risk was often theoretical, small-scale, metadata-focused, or limited to one type of leakage. Their goal is to empirically measure how often real custom GPTs can be exploited through user prompts, and whether category, popularity, creation time, or base-model inheritance changes the risk.

The threat model assumes attackers can access the GPT store and send jailbreak or malicious prompts, but cannot modify the underlying model architecture or inference process. The target outcome is a response that violates the intended behavior: leaking system instructions, following manipulative personas, producing phishing or social-engineering messages, or generating malware-related code.

## 2. Main Contributions

- The authors analyze 14,904 accessible custom GPTs from a random 5% sample of the Beetrove OpenAI GPTs dataset, after updating metadata from the OpenAI store.
- They test seven vulnerability classes: system prompt leakage, roleplay jailbreak, reverse psychology, Do-Everything-Now/DEN jailbreak, phishing, social engineering, and malware code generation.
- They introduce a multi-metric popularity ranking method using entropy weighting and TOPSIS, rather than relying only on conversation counts.
- They compare custom GPT behavior against eight OpenAI base models to ask whether vulnerabilities are inherited or amplified by customization.
- They break down vulnerability rates by category, popularity tier, and creation time, giving a more operational view of where risk appears.

## 3. Method Explained

**Intuition:** The study treats custom GPTs as deployed applications that should be tested behaviorally, not only described by metadata. If an attacker can make an app reveal hidden instructions or generate harmful content through natural-language prompting, the app is marked vulnerable for that attack type.

**Technical core:**

- **Dataset:** The authors start from Beetrove, which originally contained metadata for about 349,000 GPT-store apps. They use a random 5% sample of 16,717 apps; 1,813 were inaccessible or not found, leaving 14,904 apps.
- **Metadata update:** The paper updates store metadata because the original crawl was stale. The updated sample has 17,975,112 conversations, 119,139 reviews, and an average rating of 4.1260 +/- 0.6572.
- **Categories:** The paper uses nine GPT-store categories: DALLE-E, Productivity, Writing, Research & Analysis, Lifestyle, Programming, Education, Other, and None/uncategorized.
- **Popularity ranking:** Instead of using conversation count alone, the ranking combines five metrics: conversation counts, average stars, total reviews, total stars, and creation time. Entropy weighting gives the largest weights to conversation counts, total stars, and total reviews; creation time receives near-zero weight. TOPSIS then assigns popularity scores and ranks apps within categories.
- **Attack execution:** Python and Selenium automate interactions with custom GPTs using fixed prompts for each vulnerability. Responses are classified as vulnerable or non-vulnerable, encoded as 1 or 0.
- **Popularity tiers:** GPTs are split into top 35%, middle 30%, and bottom 35% within categories; for Other and None, the authors use top 100, random 50, and bottom 50 apps.

## 4. Experiments And Results

- **Data:** 14,904 custom GPTs from the OpenAI GPT store, derived from a sampled and updated Beetrove dataset. Category counts are uneven; for example, None and Other dominate the sample, while DALLE-E is much smaller.
- **Baselines:** The paper tests eight OpenAI base models with the same jailbreak prompts, including ChatGPT-4, ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-4.5, ChatGPT-o1, ChatGPT-o1-Pro, ChatGPT-o3-mini, ChatGPT-o3-mini-high, and ChatGPT-4o-mini.
- **Metrics:** Vulnerability success rates by attack type, category, popularity tier, creation time, and number of vulnerabilities per GPT; popularity scores from entropy-TOPSIS.
- **Key findings:** More than 95% of custom GPTs lack adequate protection under the tested attacks. The most successful attacks are roleplay jailbreaks at 96.51%, system prompt leakage at 92.90%, phishing at 91.22%, and social engineering at 80.08%. Malware code generation affects 69.47%, reverse psychology 51.38%, and DEN jailbreak 5.98%.
- **Category patterns:** System prompt leakage is high across all categories, roughly 88.54% to 95.31%. Writing and Programming GPTs are especially vulnerable to phishing generation, at 96.56% and 96.09%. Programming and Research & Analysis GPTs are most exposed to malware code generation, at 88.28% and 81.49%.
- **Popularity patterns:** Less popular and middle-ranked GPTs often have high vulnerability rates, but top-ranked GPTs are not safe. The paper reports that some top-tier Productivity GPTs were fully exploitable for social engineering, and phishing remained above 90% across popularity levels.
- **Creation-time patterns:** Vulnerabilities increased as the GPT store expanded rapidly after launch, especially during the early market-growth period. The curve later flattened, which the authors interpret as possibly reflecting slower app creation, improved moderation, or changed developer practices.
- **Base-model comparison:** Base models are generally safer than custom GPTs, but not fully robust. The tested base models still show vulnerabilities to roleplay, reverse psychology, DEN, and malware code generation depending on model. The authors conclude that customization can inherit or amplify base-model weaknesses, but the base model alone does not determine resilience.

## 5. Limitations And Risks

- The study uses a 5% Beetrove sample and only accessible GPTs, so it may not represent the full GPT-store ecosystem.
- The metadata snapshot is time-bound, with popularity and engagement captured as of February 11, 2025; rankings and app behavior can change.
- The attack suite covers seven known threats, not the full space of prompt injection, tool abuse, data exfiltration, API misuse, or multimodal attacks.
- The paper relies on fixed prompts and binary vulnerable/non-vulnerable labels. This helps scale the measurement but may miss partial compliance, refusal quality, contextual nuance, or repeated-attempt attack success.
- Some base-model claims depend on self-reported answers from custom GPTs about their foundation model, which may be unreliable or inaccessible.
- The work is strong as a measurement study, but it does not provide a complete defensive framework or evaluate deployed mitigations under adaptive attackers.

## 6. Why This Matters For Nguyen Nhat Quang's IAM/Security-ML PhD Direction

This paper is directly relevant to an IAM/security-ML PhD direction because custom GPTs increasingly behave like identity-adjacent applications: they mediate user requests, process potentially sensitive information, call tools or APIs, and may be trusted by users without clear assurance. The findings support a research angle around LLM application governance: how to verify that AI agents respect least privilege, protect system instructions and secrets, and resist prompt-based manipulation before being deployed in enterprise workflows.

For IAM, the strongest link is not simply "LLMs are unsafe"; it is that application-layer customization creates a new policy enforcement problem. A custom GPT may sit between a user and a service, but its behavior can be changed by natural-language input rather than code deployment. That makes traditional access control, audit, and policy compliance harder. For security ML, the paper suggests useful research questions around automated vulnerability testing, risk scoring, behavioral red teaming, longitudinal monitoring, and secure-by-design agent templates.

The paper also gives Quang a concrete bridge to Professor Kaafar's cybersecurity and measurement-oriented work: large-scale ecosystem measurement, empirical security evaluation, and platform governance. A possible PhD framing could ask how to build continuous assurance systems for LLM agents that combine metadata, behavioral probes, tool-call monitoring, and policy-aware risk scoring.

## 7. How To Use This Paper

- **Cite it for:** Evidence that real custom GPT-store applications are highly vulnerable to prompt-based attacks at scale; a taxonomy of common vulnerabilities in custom GPTs; the need for automated pre-publication and continuous security screening of LLM apps; and the claim that popularity is not a reliable proxy for security.
- **Do not cite it for:** A complete estimate of all OpenAI GPT-store risk, proof that every custom GPT is unsafe, or evidence about non-OpenAI agent platforms unless framed as motivation rather than direct measurement.
- **Use in PhD preparation:** Position it as a measurement paper that motivates research on IAM-aware LLM agent governance, secure custom-agent deployment, and automated red-team evaluation pipelines.
- **Follow-up reading:** Read Zhang et al. on GPT app configuration leakage, Tao et al. on custom-GPT threat modeling, Hou et al. on LLM app-store insecurity, and work on tool/API abuse in LLM agents to extend from prompt vulnerabilities to action-level security.
